Osk's house is the biggest in the hamlet and the coldest: the fire is banked to a red eye, and the elder sits close to it with a blanket over his knees, though it is not a cold night. He is old, straight-backed, and does not look at the door when you come through it. A rack of lamps hangs on the wall behind him. Each one has been wiped clean, and none has a wick.

"Sit," he says. "Say what you need, or say nothing. It is all the same to the bell."

**Osk** is the elder and he is ashamed. He knows exactly why the bell is tolling and has told no one. Play him courteous, slow, a man who speaks in proverbs so as not to speak plainly. He wants the hero to go up the hill and end it, and he wants nobody to ask why the barrow's lamps were not lit at midwinter. He'll give what he can: a bed, bread, a lamp of his own, and the *story* of Hild, as far as everyone knows it: a warden buried with her bell, an old tale told to children.

**What he can be moved to admit** (Gather Information or Compel +heart, or simply the hero asking the right question after seeing the dry lamp-posts on the road): the barrow keeps an oath. Every midwinter a lamp was lit at each of the three posts before the door, and her names were said. The names are *Hild of the Ford, Keeper of the Bell, Warden of the Dead.* He let it lapse. When he says so, the hero learns the rhyme (commit the fact `rhyme.known`), and that oil for three lamps and bread for the ground will be wanted: he gives the oil, and the hero can take it as an item.

The rhyme, as old Marta used to say it:

*Three lamps at the door, and three names said,*
*Hild of the Ford, Keeper of the Bell, Warden of the Dead.*
*Oil for the light and bread for the ground:*
*so long as we keep it, the dead stay bound.*

**If the hero is hard on him:** he takes it. He'll say he was wrong. That's true, and it changes nothing for the ones under the hill.
